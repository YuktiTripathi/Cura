## How to read this tab

Each topic is library content, not a script for one answer. It opens with how the body works, then what the test shows, what each result may mean, which results it is read with and why, what can influence it, and how to look after that part of health. Sources are linked under each topic, and every claim is mapped in the Evidence tab. Nothing here is clinically approved.

**Looking after your health** follows the same four steps in every topic, and a step is left out when it does not apply:

- **Keeping it healthy:** for a result in range. It says what is encouraging and what the test cannot promise.
- **Improving it:** for a result outside range, where habits are known to help.
- **Lowering your risk:** for a raised Bio-AI prediction. Only risk factors a person can change.
- **When to see a doctor:** when habits are not enough, or the result needs a cause found.

## Liver Health

**How the body works.** After a meal, your stomach and intestines break food into tiny pieces your body can absorb. The blood leaving them flows to the liver first, before it reaches the rest of the body. Your liver sits under your right ribs and works like a busy kitchen and cleaning centre in one. It stores sugar to release between meals, builds proteins your blood needs, and turns medicines and alcohol into forms the body can use or remove. Most fats from food take a different route, through small vessels called lymph vessels, and reach the liver later.

The liver also makes bile, a yellow-green liquid that helps break fat into small droplets so it can be digested. Bile drains from the liver through small tubes called bile ducts into the gut. It also carries waste out of the body, including bilirubin, a yellow pigment left over when old red blood cells are recycled.

**What the tests show.** No single test tells you how the liver is doing. Each one looks at a different job:

- **ALT and AST** are enzymes, proteins that help chemical changes happen inside cells. They sit inside liver cells, and leak into the blood when those cells are irritated or damaged. AST is also found in muscle.
- **ALP and GGT** are enzymes found along the bile ducts. They tend to rise when bile is not draining freely. ALP is also made by bone, and GGT is not.
- **Bilirubin** shows whether the liver is keeping up with clearing that yellow pigment.
- **Albumin** is a protein the liver builds. It shows part of the liver's building work, though illness and poor nutrition also lower it.

**How the results connect.** Doctors look at the pattern, not one number. Raised ALT and AST point to strain on liver cells. Raised ALP and GGT point to bile not draining well. Raised bilirubin as well adds weight to either pattern. An abnormal result does not always mean liver disease, and other tests are usually needed to find a cause.

**Looking after your liver.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** results in range are encouraging for the jobs these tests check. They cannot check every part of the liver. Fat can build up with normal ALT, for example.
- **Lowering the strain:** alcohol has to be broken down by the liver, so drinking less, or not at all, reduces its workload. Extra fat around the waist and diets heavy in sugar and refined flour (maida) make fat build up inside the liver. Regular activity and a healthy waist size help. Some herbal and dietary supplements, including some sold for bodybuilding or weight loss, can occasionally injure the liver, so check new ones with a doctor.
- **When to see a doctor:** yellow eyes or skin, dark urine, pale stools, itching, swelling of the tummy, or results that stay abnormal on a repeat test.

Fatty liver is common in India. A review of Indian studies found it in about one in three adults, and in South Asia a large share of people with it are not obese.

**Questions people ask.** "What can I do to support my liver health?" "Can I have liver problems if my tests are normal?" "Which of my liver results matter most?"

## Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)

**How the body works.** Every moment, your cells are building, breaking down and changing substances. Most of these chemical changes would be far too slow on their own, so the body uses enzymes, proteins that speed them up, a bit like a catalyst in cooking. ALP is one such enzyme. It is found in many tissues, but mostly in two places: the liver, especially along the bile ducts that drain bile, and bone.

Bone is not fixed. Throughout life, old bone is cleared away and new bone is laid down. The cells that build new bone use ALP, so more bone-building means more ALP in the blood. In the liver, ALP sits along the bile ducts, and more of it reaches the blood when bile does not drain freely.

**What the test shows.** The amount of ALP in your blood. It is a clue to what is happening in the bile ducts or in bone, but it cannot say which on its own.

**Your result.**

- **In range:** reassuring for bile drainage and bone activity at the time of the test. It is not a check of every liver job, and an early change can still sit inside the range.
- **Higher:** usually either bile not draining freely (from a gallstone, or swelling or other problems in the liver) or more bone-building. More bone-building is normal in growing children and teenagers, in pregnancy and while a fracture heals. It can also happen with an overactive thyroid or parathyroid gland, or with bone conditions such as osteomalacia, a softening of the bones usually caused by low vitamin D. A slightly high result on its own, with nothing else abnormal, may not be a problem.
- **Lower:** uncommon. It can go with poor nutrition or low protein, low zinc, thyroid disease, pernicious anaemia (a condition that stops the body absorbing B12), Wilson disease, or a rare inherited bone condition called hypophosphatasia. It is worth mentioning to a doctor rather than ignoring.

**Read together with.** GGT is the most useful companion. It is found in the liver and bile ducts but not in bone. If ALP and GGT are both high, the source is probably the liver. If ALP is high and GGT is normal, bone becomes more likely. This is a strong clue, not proof. Bilirubin helps too: if bile is backing up, bilirubin may rise as well. Vitamin D and calcium results can help when bone is the likely source. When the source stays unclear, a doctor can order a test that separates the liver and bone forms of ALP.

**What can influence it.** These are general influences, not the confirmed reason for any one person's result.

- **Age and growth:** bones that are still growing make much more ALP, so children's ranges are higher.
- **Pregnancy:** ALP is normally higher during pregnancy.
- **Low vitamin D:** the body needs vitamin D to keep enough calcium available for bones. A long shortage can soften bones (osteomalacia), which is one cause of a high ALP.
- **Medicines:** many can change ALP, so a doctor needs the full list.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** there is no habit that targets ALP itself. The general liver and bone habits apply: limit alcohol, stay active, and get enough calcium (milk, curd, paneer, ragi, sesame) and some daylight for vitamin D.
- **When to see a doctor:** a raised ALP needs its cause found, especially with yellow eyes or skin, itching, dark urine, pale stools or bone pain. Habits will not correct it until the cause is known.

**Questions people ask.** "Is my high ALP from my liver or my bones?" "Can low vitamin D raise ALP?" "Should I worry about a low ALP?"

## Direct bilirubin

**How the body works.** Red blood cells carry oxygen around your body, and each one lasts about three to four months. Every day, some old ones are broken down and replaced. Breaking them down leaves behind bilirubin, a yellow pigment. Bilirubin cannot leave the body as it is, so it travels in the blood to the liver. The liver changes it into a form that dissolves in bile, and bile carries it into the gut and out in stool. Bilirubin is what gives stool its brown colour.

**What the test shows.** Reports usually give two numbers. Total bilirubin is all the bilirubin in the blood. Direct bilirubin is mainly the form the liver has already processed. The difference between the two is called indirect bilirubin, the part not yet processed.

**Your result.**

- **In range:** reassuring for this part of bilirubin handling. It cannot rule out every bile duct or liver problem, because an early or partial blockage may not raise it yet.
- **Higher:** processed bilirubin is getting back into the blood instead of draining out with bile. Common reasons are a blockage of a bile duct (often a gallstone), inflammation of the bile ducts, or liver conditions such as hepatitis. When enough bilirubin builds up, skin and eyes turn yellow, which is called jaundice.
- **Lower:** not a concern.

**Read together with.** The pattern between total and direct bilirubin points to where the problem is. If total bilirubin is high mostly because of the direct part, the liver has processed bilirubin but it is not draining out, which points to the bile ducts or liver. If total bilirubin is high but direct is normal, the liver has not processed it yet. That usually points to faster red cell breakdown, or Gilbert syndrome, a common inherited pattern that is not dangerous and can make eyes look slightly yellow with fasting or poor sleep. ALP and GGT add weight when bile drainage is the issue, and ALT and AST show whether liver cells are strained.

**What can influence it.** Many medicines can change bilirubin, so a doctor needs the full list. Bilirubin breaks down in light, so a sample left in light can read falsely low.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** no habit targets direct bilirubin. General liver care applies.
- **When to see a doctor:** soon for any raised direct bilirubin. Seek care promptly for yellow eyes or skin, dark urine, pale or clay-coloured stools, itching, fever or pain under the right ribs. This needs a cause found, not a change in habits.

**Limit.** Labs report the processed form as "direct" bilirubin because of the way it is measured. The measurement does not match the processed form exactly, so results can differ slightly between labs.

**Questions people ask.** "Should I be concerned about my bilirubin result?" "What is the difference between direct and total bilirubin?" "Could a gallstone raise my bilirubin?"

Sources: [MedlinePlus, bilirubin blood test](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/bilirubin-blood-test) · [MedlinePlus, bilirubin (encyclopaedia)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003479.htm) · [Harvard Health, high bilirubin](https://www.health.harvard.edu/digestive-health/what-does-a-high-bilirubin-level-in-my-blood-test-actually-mean) · [MedlinePlus, bilirubin in urine](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003595.htm)

## Vitamin B12

**How the body works.** Your body is always making new cells. Your bone marrow, the soft tissue inside bones, makes a huge number of new red blood cells every day. To copy itself, each new cell needs DNA, the instruction code inside every cell, and making DNA needs vitamin B12. Nerves need B12 too. Nerves are the wiring that carries signals between your brain and the rest of your body, and B12 helps keep that wiring healthy.

Your body cannot make B12, so it must come from food. It occurs naturally only in animal foods: milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish and meat. Plant foods contain none unless B12 is added. Absorbing it takes two steps. Stomach acid first frees B12 from food, then it joins a protein made by the stomach, called intrinsic factor, so the gut can take it in. The liver stores a reserve, which is why a shortage can take months or years to appear.

**What the test shows.** The amount of B12 in your blood. It is a good guide, but it does not always show how much B12 your cells can actually use.

**Your result.**

- **In range:** you have enough B12 in your blood at the moment. A result near the bottom of the range can still go with a mild shortage, so symptoms such as tingling still matter.
- **Lower:** usually either too little B12 in the diet, or difficulty absorbing it. Absorption can fall with age, with stomach conditions that reduce acid or intrinsic factor, with gut conditions such as coeliac disease, and with medicines such as metformin (a diabetes medicine) and long-term acid-reducing tablets. That is why people who eat meat can be low too. A shortage can cause tiredness, weakness, pale skin, tingling or numbness in the hands and feet, and memory problems. Nerve damage can happen even before the blood count changes.
- **Higher:** B12 itself has not been shown to cause harm, even at high doses, and supplements are the usual reason. A high level without supplements is different. It can be a sign of liver disease, kidney disease or some blood conditions, so it is worth discussing with a doctor.

**Read together with.** Haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in red cells, and MCV, the average size of red cells. Without enough B12, cells cannot divide properly and grow larger than usual, so a high MCV with low haemoglobin fits a B12 shortage. A doctor may also check folate, another B vitamin, when red cells are large. Normal blood counts do not rule out a B12 shortage that is affecting the nerves.

**What can influence it.** General influences, not the confirmed reason for any one result:

- **Eating little or no dairy, eggs, fish or meat:** less B12 comes in. Low B12 is common in India, partly because many people are vegetarian.
- **Age and stomach health:** less stomach acid or intrinsic factor means less B12 absorbed.
- **Metformin and acid-reducing tablets:** both can lower absorption over time.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** regular B12 foods, such as milk, curd, paneer or eggs daily if you eat them, or B12-fortified foods if you are vegan.
- **Improving it:** more B12 foods help when the diet is the reason. They do not help when absorption is the problem, which is why a clearly low result needs a doctor. Doctors often treat a shortage with tablets or injections; CURA does not suggest products or doses.
- **When to see a doctor:** any clearly low result, numbness, tingling, balance or memory problems, or a high result without supplements.

**Questions people ask.** "Can my vitamin B12 be low even if I eat non-vegetarian food?" "Which vegetarian foods have B12?" "Could my diabetes medicine affect my B12?"

Sources: [NIH ODS, vitamin B12 for consumers](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/) · [NIH ODS, B12 for health professionals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/) · [B12 status in Indian adults, review](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnsv/66/Supplement/66_S361/_article/-char/ja) · [Maternal B12 in India, systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8074968) · [Elevated B12 in clinical practice, QJM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23447660/) · [High B12 and disease, PMC3448722](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3448722)

## HDL cholesterol

**How the body works.** Cholesterol is a waxy fat, and your body needs some of it. Every cell uses it to build its outer wall, and the body uses it to make some hormones (chemical messages carried in the blood) and vitamin D. Your liver makes most of the cholesterol you need, and some comes from food.

Blood is mostly water, and fat does not mix with water, so cholesterol cannot float freely in it. It travels inside tiny carrier particles instead. Two of these matter most. LDL particles deliver cholesterol out to the body; when there is too much, some gets stuck in artery walls and builds up as plaque, which narrows arteries over years. HDL particles do the opposite job. They collect spare cholesterol and carry it back to the liver, which removes it from the body. That clean-up job is why HDL is called good cholesterol.

**What the test shows.** How much cholesterol is being carried in HDL particles. It does not show how well those particles do their clean-up job, which can vary from person to person.

**Your result.**

- **In range:** encouraging for this part of your cholesterol profile. People with naturally higher HDL tend to have somewhat lower heart risk. HDL is only one part of heart risk, though, and a good HDL does not cancel out a high LDL.
- **Lower:** linked with a higher chance of heart disease. Low activity, smoking, extra weight, diabetes and a diet high in refined carbohydrates such as sugar, white bread and maida tend to lower HDL. Genes also matter.
- **Higher:** usually favourable. Very high levels, above about 100 mg/dL, do not add extra protection.

**Read together with.** LDL, triglycerides (the main fat the body stores for energy) and non-HDL cholesterol (everything except HDL). Low HDL with high triglycerides is a common pattern in South Asians and is linked with insulin resistance, when the body's cells stop responding well to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar into them. If LDL is high and HDL is low, doctors usually focus on lowering LDL first. Raising HDL on its own is not a treatment goal.

**What can influence it.** General influences, not the confirmed reason for one person's HDL:

- **Physical activity:** regular movement raises HDL and lowers triglycerides.
- **Smoking:** lowers HDL.
- **Weight around the waist and insulin resistance:** tend to lower HDL and raise triglycerides.
- **Genes and ancestry:** South Asians tend to have lower HDL, and their HDL particles tend to be smaller and work less well.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** if you are already active and do not smoke, those habits are doing useful work.
- **Improving it:** about 30 minutes of brisk walking, cycling or other moderate activity on most days, stopping smoking, and replacing sugary snacks and maida with whole grains, dal, nuts and vegetables. Medicines that raise HDL have not been shown to lower heart risk, so lifestyle is the main route. Do not start drinking alcohol to raise HDL.
- **When to see a doctor:** low HDL together with high LDL, high triglycerides, diabetes, high blood pressure, or heart disease in close family at a young age.

**Questions people ask.** "Why is my good cholesterol low?" "Does a good HDL protect me if my LDL is high?" "What does my cholesterol say about my heart health?"

Sources: [MedlinePlus, HDL](https://medlineplus.gov/hdlthegoodcholesterol.html) · [Mayo Clinic, HDL cholesterol](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/high-blood-cholesterol/in-depth/hdl-cholesterol/art-20046388) · [Harvard Health, HDL is complex](https://www.health.harvard.edu/newsletter_article/hdl-the-good-but-complex-cholesterol) · [Dyslipidaemia in South Asians, PMC4807313](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4807313) · [Indian dyslipidaemia, PMC4297876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297876)

## HbA1c

**How the body works.** Foods such as rice, roti, idli, potatoes, fruit and sugar contain carbohydrates. Your gut breaks them down into glucose, a sugar every cell uses as fuel. Glucose travels in the blood, and after a meal the level rises. Your pancreas, an organ behind the stomach, then releases insulin. Insulin is a hormone, a chemical message, that tells cells to take glucose in from the blood, so the level falls again. If cells respond poorly to insulin, or the pancreas cannot make enough, glucose stays high in the blood.

While glucose is in the blood, some of it sticks to haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein inside red blood cells. The more glucose there is, and the longer it stays high, the more sticks. Red blood cells last about three months.

**What the test shows.** HbA1c is the percentage of haemoglobin with glucose attached. Because red cells last about three months, it reflects your average blood sugar over roughly the past two to three months. You do not need to fast for it.

**Your result.** Common cut-offs are: below 5.7% normal, 5.7% to 6.4% the prediabetes range, and 6.5% or above the diabetes range. Your lab's own range applies, and a doctor confirms any diagnosis, often with a second test.

- **In range:** your average blood sugar has been healthy over recent months. It does not show short spikes after meals.
- **In the prediabetes range:** blood sugar has been running higher than healthy, but below the diabetes level. Prediabetes raises the chance of type 2 diabetes and heart disease. Lifestyle changes can delay or prevent type 2 diabetes.
- **In the diabetes range:** this needs a doctor to confirm and to plan care. Over years, high blood sugar can damage the heart, kidneys, eyes and nerves.
- **Lower than range:** usually not a concern. If you take diabetes medicines, ask your doctor what a low result means for you.

**Read together with.** Fasting blood sugar is a single snapshot after a night without food, while HbA1c is the longer view, so the two can differ. Haemoglobin matters too. Very low iron can make HbA1c read falsely high. Recent blood loss, kidney failure and some inherited types of haemoglobin can also make it read too high or too low.

**What can influence it.** General influences, not the confirmed reason for one person's result:

- **Meal pattern and portion size:** large portions of rice, roti or maida, sweets and sugary drinks raise blood sugar more.
- **Physical activity:** helps manage blood sugar, and a reading after a walk is often lower than before. Not being active is a risk factor for insulin resistance.
- **Weight around the waist:** linked with cells responding less to insulin.
- **Family history and age:** risk rises with both, and is higher in South Asians.
- **Red cell conditions:** these change the test itself, not your blood sugar.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** if your meals already include plenty of vegetables, dal or other protein and you move most days, those habits support healthy blood sugar.
- **Improving it:** fill half your plate with vegetables and add dal, curd, paneer, eggs, fish or chicken; take smaller portions of white rice and maida; swap sweet drinks for water, buttermilk or unsweetened chai; and build regular activity into the day. These steps help many people, but they do not work the same for everyone.
- **When to see a doctor:** any result in the prediabetes or diabetes range, so a doctor can confirm it and decide what follow-up and treatment is needed. Get care promptly for extreme thirst, passing a lot of urine, unexplained weight loss or blurred vision.

**Questions people ask.** "What does my HbA1c say about my diabetes risk?" "How long does it take for HbA1c to improve?" "Why can my HbA1c and fasting sugar disagree?"

## ESR

**How the body works.** When you cut your finger or catch an infection, your body sends defenders to the spot. More blood flows there and immune cells gather to fight germs and start repairs. That is why a cut turns red, warm and swollen. This protective response is called inflammation. It is part of how you heal, and it usually settles once the problem is dealt with. Sometimes inflammation continues for a long time, for example when the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own joints or tissues. That is called an autoimmune condition.

During inflammation, the liver releases extra proteins into the blood. Some of these make red blood cells stick together in small stacks.

**What the test shows.** The lab places your blood in a tall, thin tube and measures how far the red cells sink in one hour. Single red cells sink slowly. Stacks of cells are heavier and sink faster. So a faster rate, a higher ESR, suggests more of those inflammation proteins. The test shows that inflammation may be present, but not where it is or what is causing it.

**Your result.**

- **In range:** this test does not show marked inflammation at the moment. It is a broad, simple test, and some inflammation can be present with a normal ESR.
- **Higher:** may mean inflammation from an infection, an autoimmune condition such as rheumatoid arthritis, or another illness. ESR can also be raised by anaemia, pregnancy, kidney disease and thyroid disease, and the normal upper limit is higher in older adults and in women. On its own it cannot point to one condition, and other tests are almost always needed.
- **Lower:** rarely important. Some conditions lower it, such as having too many red cells (polycythaemia), sickle cell anaemia or heart failure.

**Read together with.** CRP (C-reactive protein) is another protein the liver releases during inflammation. Its level rises and falls with the amount of inflammation, and doctors often order it with ESR for more information. Haemoglobin matters because anaemia can raise ESR without inflammation. The white cell count can show whether the body is fighting an infection.

**What can influence it.** General influences: age, sex, pregnancy, anaemia, recent infection or injury, and some medicines.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** no habit targets ESR. A normal result needs no change.
- **When to see a doctor:** a raised ESR needs its cause found, not lifestyle change, especially with unexplained fever, joint pain, muscle symptoms or a new headache. Doctors also use ESR to follow conditions already diagnosed.

**Questions people ask.** "Why is my ESR high?" "How is ESR different from CRP?" "Does a normal ESR mean I have no inflammation?"

## Oxidative stress (Bio-AI)

**How the body works.** Your cells turn food into energy using oxygen, a little like a fire burning fuel. Just as a fire throws off sparks, this process naturally throws off small, very reactive molecules, often called free radicals. Exercise makes some, and so do outside sources such as cigarette smoke, air pollution and strong sunlight. In small amounts these molecules are normal and even useful. In large amounts they can damage parts of cells, including DNA, the cell's instruction code.

Your body has its own protection. It makes protective proteins that neutralise these molecules, and antioxidants from food, found in vegetables, fruit and grains, add to that defence. Oxidative stress means the reactive molecules are outpacing the protection.

**Why it matters.** Researchers think long-lasting oxidative stress may play a part in conditions such as heart disease, diabetes and some cancers. This link is still being studied. It is not proven to cause these conditions in any one person.

**What the prediction shows.** Your Bio-AI assessment estimates how likely this imbalance is, on the provider's own scale. MetSights draws on blood results, food and lifestyle habits and questionnaire answers to make its estimates, but it does not say which inputs shaped your score. The estimate is not a measurement of damage in your body, and a score is not a percentage chance. Routine blood tests do not measure oxidative stress directly, so a normal blood report and a raised estimate can sit side by side.

**What can influence it.** General influences studied in this area, not the confirmed reasons for anyone's score:

- **Smoking and second-hand smoke:** a strong outside source of reactive molecules.
- **Air pollution:** another outside source.
- **Diets low in vegetables and fruit:** less antioxidant help from food.

**Looking after it.**

- **Lowering your risk:** not smoking, and eating a variety of vegetables, fruit, dal and whole grains, are the best-supported steps. For example, add a sabzi and a piece of seasonal fruit to your day. Regular, moderate activity is part of a healthy routine. Antioxidant supplements have not been shown to prevent disease, and some high-dose supplements can cause harm, such as high-dose beta-carotene raising lung cancer risk in smokers.
- **When to see a doctor:** there is no specific test or treatment for oxidative stress. Talk to a doctor if other raised risks or symptoms worry you.

**Questions people ask.** "Why is my oxidative stress risk high when my blood tests look fine?" "Do I need antioxidant supplements?" "Which foods help my body's own protection?"

## Fatty liver (Bio-AI)

**How the body works.** After a meal, your body stores spare energy for later. Some is stored as fat, mostly under the skin and around the organs. A healthy liver holds only a little fat. When the body takes in more sugar and fat than it can use, and especially when cells respond poorly to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar into cells, more fat ends up stored inside the liver itself. When this is not caused by heavy drinking, doctors now call it MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease). It was earlier called NAFLD.

**Why it matters.** Fatty liver often causes no symptoms, even for years. In some people the extra fat leads to inflammation and damage. This is called MASH, and over time it can scar the liver (fibrosis) and, in some, lead to severe scarring (cirrhosis) or liver cancer. How a person feels does not show whether this is happening, which is why it is worth assessing. Fatty liver is also linked with type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and abnormal blood fats. It is common in India: about one in three adults in a review of Indian studies. In South Asia, a large share of people with fatty liver are not obese.

**What the prediction shows.** The Bio-AI score estimates how likely fatty liver is, using blood results, food and lifestyle habits and questionnaire answers. It cannot see the liver. An ultrasound or other scan shows fat, and special scans can measure scarring. Liver blood tests answer a different question: ALT and AST show whether liver cells are strained, and they can be normal in fatty liver. So a normal ALT does not rule fatty liver out, and a lower score does not make an abnormal ALT unimportant.

**What can influence it.** General influences, not confirmed reasons for anyone's score:

- **Extra weight, especially around the waist:** linked with more fat stored in the liver.
- **Type 2 diabetes or high blood sugar, and high triglycerides:** these go with insulin resistance, which pushes fat into the liver.
- **Diet:** sugary drinks, sweets and refined flour (maida) in large amounts add to the load.
- **Genes:** some people develop fatty liver without any of these.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** regular activity and meals built around vegetables, dal and whole grains support a healthy liver.
- **Lowering your risk:** losing extra weight gradually helps. Even 3 to 5% of body weight can reduce liver fat, and larger losses help more. Regular physical activity helps the liver even when weight does not change. Cut back on sugary drinks and sweets, and limit or avoid alcohol. Fatty liver is one of the conditions where everyday habits make a real difference, though they cannot undo advanced scarring.
- **When to see a doctor:** a raised or very high estimate, abnormal liver tests, or yellow eyes, swelling of the tummy or legs, or itching. A doctor may suggest an ultrasound.

**Questions people ask.** "Can I have fatty liver with normal liver tests?" "Can fatty liver be reversed?" "Why do I have fatty liver if I don't drink?"

## Metabolic age (Bio-AI)

**How the body works.** Everything your body does runs on energy from food. After a meal, carbohydrates become glucose, a sugar your cells use as fuel. Insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas, moves glucose out of the blood into cells, keeping blood sugar within a workable range. Fats from food are carried in the blood, used for energy or stored for later, and your liver keeps the balance between storing and releasing. Together, these steps are called metabolism: how the body turns food into energy and uses or stores it. When metabolism is working well, blood sugar and blood fats stay in healthy ranges and the body copes easily with meals. When cells in the muscles, fat and liver stop responding well to insulin, called insulin resistance, these steps work less well. Inactivity and extra weight raise the chance of this, while a healthy weight, regular activity and enough sleep are part of the lifestyle that may help prevent it.

**What the estimate is.** Metabolic age is an estimate from MetSights that summarises parts of your metabolic health and expresses it as an age. MetSights can draw on blood results, food and lifestyle habits, activity, sleep, demographic details and questionnaire answers when it builds such scores. It has not published its formula, the weight it gives each input, or the group it compares people with. So CURA cannot say what raised or lowered your estimate, or exactly what it is compared against.

**What it is not.** It is not your biological age, the age of your organs, how fast you are ageing, or how long you will live. It is not a diagnosis, and a missing estimate does not mean your metabolic health is normal.

**Your estimate.**

- **Close to or below your actual age:** encouraging as a summary. Your measured blood sugar, HbA1c, triglycerides and HDL still matter on their own.
- **Above your actual age:** a prompt to look at metabolic health, not a verdict. Your measured results show where attention would help most.

**Read together with.** Your fasting blood sugar and HbA1c (how the body handles sugar), triglycerides and HDL (how it handles fats), and waist size if recorded. These are measured; metabolic age is estimated. CURA must not say these results caused the estimate, because the provider does not say which inputs it used for you.

**Looking after it.**

- **Keeping it healthy:** regular movement, including some strength exercise; meals built around vegetables, dal and whole grains; and regular sleep. If your questionnaire answers show you already do these, keep going.
- **Improving metabolic health:** work on the specific measured results that are outside range, such as blood sugar or triglycerides, using the guidance for those tests. A later estimate may change, but no habit is promised to change it by a set number of years.
- **When to see a doctor:** there is no treatment for metabolic age itself. See a doctor about any measured result outside range.

**Questions people ask.** "Can my metabolic age change over time?" "How is metabolic age different from biological age?" "Which of my results matter most for my metabolic health?"
